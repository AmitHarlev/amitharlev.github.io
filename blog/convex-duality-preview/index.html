What is the dual of a linear program?

September 8, 2026

Imagine you have a solution to the following LP and you want to prove to your boss that it is optimal.

$$ \begin{array}{rl} \text{maximize} & x_1+2x_2 \\ \text{subject to} & -x_1+4x_2\le24, \\ & x_1+x_2\le9, \\ & 2x_1-2x_2\le11, \\ & 2x_1+3x_2\le28, \\ & x_1,x_2\ge0. \end{array} $$

You know the optimal solution is $x_1^* = 2.4$ and $x_2^* = 6.6$ because you ran the simplex algorithm. However, your boss is worried you had a bug in your code and wants a quick and easy way to certify that this solution is actually optimal. How might you do this?

One way is to find an upper bound on the optimal value of this LP. If we can show that $x_1^* + 2x_2^* = 15.6$ is an upper bound, that would prove our solution is optimal (because we have a solution that is as good as the upper bound). That just shifts the question: how do we find an upper bound?

If you look at the set of constraints, you will notice that one of them is $2x_1+3x_2\le28$. Since we are only considering $x_1, x_2 \geq 0$, it follows that $2x_1 + 3x_2 \geq x_1 + 2x_2$, which is our objective. Thus, it must be that $x_1 + 2x_2 \leq 28$ and so $28$ is an upper bound on the optimal value. Unfortunately, this doesn't help us prove our solution is optimal, or even close to optimal, since $28$ is a lot bigger than $15.6$. But we can find a tighter upper bound!

We have the constraint $x_1+x_2\le9$. If we double both sides, we get $2x_1+2x_2\le18$, which by the same reasoning as above means that $18$ is an upper bound. This is a noticable improvement! While we still haven't proven that our solution is optimal, we have proven that our solution is at most $13.4\%$ below the optimal value. However, we are confident that our solution is optimal because we ran the simplex algorithm (and we're sure there were no bugs in the code)! Can we find an even tighter upper bound?

Yes! We can scale two of our constraints and then add them:

$$ \begin{array}{r@{\quad}rcl} \frac14\times & -x_1+4x_2 & \le & 24 \\ \frac54\times & x_1+x_2 & \le & 9 \\ \hline & x_1+\frac94x_2 & \le & 17.25 \end{array} $$

Since $x_2\ge0$, we have $x_1+2x_2\le x_1+\frac94x_2\le17.25$. Thus, $17.25$ is an upper bound, getting us very close to $15.6$! We could keep trying different combinations to get a bound better than $17.25$, but it may become hard to eyeball ways to scale and combine our constraints so that they yield progressively tighter upper bounds. Instead, let's ask: what is the best possible bound we can get using this technique?

We are looking for weights $y_1,y_2,y_3,y_4 \geq 0$ so that, after scaling and adding our constraints, the coefficient of $x_1$ is at least $1$ and the coefficient of $x_2$ is at least $2$:

$$ \begin{array}{r@{\quad}rcl} y_1\times & -x_1+4x_2 & \le & 24 \\ y_2\times & x_1+x_2 & \le & 9 \\ y_3\times & 2x_1-2x_2 & \le & 11 \\ y_4\times & 2x_1+3x_2 & \le & 28 \\ \hline & (-y_1+y_2+2y_3+2y_4)x_1+(4y_1+y_2-2y_3+3y_4)x_2 & \le & 24y_1+9y_2+11y_3+28y_4 \end{array} $$

Using the exact same reasoning as before, we can conclude that for any choice of $y_1,y_2,y_3,y_4 \geq 0$ such that

$$ \begin{aligned} -y_1+y_2+2y_3+2y_4 &\ge 1, \\ 4y_1+y_2-2y_3+3y_4 &\ge 2, \end{aligned} $$

the right-hand side of the above inequality, $24y_1+9y_2+11y_3+28y_4$, is an upper bound on the optimal value. So we want to choose $y_1, y_2, y_3, y_4 \geq 0$ so as to minimize $24y_1+9y_2+11y_3+28y_4$ while ensuring that both the above inequalities are satisfied. This is just another linear program!

$$ \begin{array}{rl} \text{minimize} & 24y_1+9y_2+11y_3+28y_4 \\ \text{subject to} & -y_1+y_2+2y_3+2y_4\ge1, \\ & 4y_1+y_2-2y_3+3y_4\ge2, \\ & y_1,y_2,y_3,y_4\ge0. \end{array} $$

The optimal value of this linear program is the best possible upper bound we can find using this approach. Solving this new LP with simplex, we find that the optimal solution is $y_1^*=\frac15$, $y_2^*=\frac65$, and $y_3^*=y_4^*=0$, giving an upper bound of $24\cdot\frac15+9\cdot\frac65=15.6$. Conveniently, this is exactly equal to our original solution, proving that it is in fact optimal! We will see later that it was not a coincidence that the best upper bound we could find by combining constraints exactly matched the optimal value of the original LP.

The approach we used here was not specific to this LP. Consider a general maximization LP with $n$ nonnegative variables and $m$ inequality constraints:

$$ \begin{array}{rl} \text{maximize} & c_1x_1+\cdots+c_nx_n \\ \text{subject to} & a_{11}x_1+\cdots+a_{1n}x_n\le b_1, \\ & \vdots \\ & a_{m1}x_1+\cdots+a_{mn}x_n\le b_m, \\ & x_1,\ldots,x_n\ge0. \end{array} $$

Here, the $a_{ij}$, $b_i$, and $c_j$ are given coefficients. As before, we assign a nonnegative weight $y_i$ to each constraint, multiply, and add:

$$ \begin{array}{r@{\quad}rcl} y_1\times & a_{11}x_1+\cdots+a_{1n}x_n & \le & b_1 \\ y_2\times & a_{21}x_1+\cdots+a_{2n}x_n & \le & b_2 \\ \vdots & \vdots & & \vdots \\ y_m\times & a_{m1}x_1+\cdots+a_{mn}x_n & \le & b_m \\ \hline & \displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(a_{1j}y_1+\cdots+a_{mj}y_m\right)x_j & \le & \displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}b_i y_i \end{array} $$

If the coefficient of each $x_j$ in this sum is at least its objective coefficient $c_j$, then, because $x_j\ge0$, the right-hand side is an upper bound on the objective. To find the smallest such bound, we solve another LP:

$$ \begin{array}{rl} \text{minimize} & b_1y_1+\cdots+b_my_m \\ \text{subject to} & a_{11}y_1+\cdots+a_{m1}y_m\ge c_1, \\ & \vdots \\ & a_{1n}y_1+\cdots+a_{mn}y_m\ge c_n, \\ & y_1,\ldots,y_m\ge0. \end{array} $$

This is called the dual LP, and the original LP is called the primal LP. Because of how we constructed the dual LP, we know that the objective value of any feasible dual solution provides an upper bound on the optimal value of the primal LP. This property is known as weak duality.

Theorem (Weak duality). For any feasible solution $x_1,\ldots,x_n$ to the primal LP and any feasible solution $y_1,\ldots,y_m$ to its dual LP,

$$ c_1x_1+\cdots+c_nx_n\le b_1y_1+\cdots+b_my_m. $$

Consequently, if both LPs have optimal solutions, the primal maximum is at most the dual minimum.

In our example we saw something even stronger: the primal maximum was exactly equal to the dual minimum. This equality always holds when the primal LP is feasible and has a finite optimal value. This result is called strong duality.

Theorem (Strong duality). If the primal LP is feasible and has a finite optimal value, then both the primal and dual LPs attain optimal solutions $x_1^*,\ldots,x_n^*$ and $y_1^*,\ldots,y_m^*$, and their optimal values are equal:

$$ c_1x_1^*+\cdots+c_nx_n^*=b_1y_1^*+\cdots+b_my_m^*. $$

Weak duality followed directly from how we constructed the dual. It is the LP that finds the minimal upper bound attainable by taking scaled sums of the constraints of the primal LP. On the other hand, thinking about the construction does not provide an obvious explanation for why strong duality holds. We will instead explain it by thinking about physics!

The Physics Intuition for Strong Duality

Imagine dropping a ball inside the feasible region of your LP, with a “gravitational” force equal to the gradient of the objective. View each constraint as a wall that the ball cannot pass through, and assume collisions are perfectly inelastic: the ball does not bounce off any wall, but can still slide along them. As is common in physics, we will assume an idealized setting where the only forces acting on the ball are "gravity" and the normal forces exerted by the walls (so no friction, air resistance, etc).

The interactive demo below simulates this physical system. Click anywhere within the feasible region to drop a ball. The grey arrows represent the gradient of the objective function, which acts as the gravitational force on the ball.

If you’ve ever dropped a ball within an enclosed space, you know that it will eventually stop moving. Altering the direction or strength of gravity will not change this fact. Thus, we expect the ball to settle at the “lowest” point under our chosen gravity, which corresponds to the highest objective value. At this equilibrium position, all the forces balance (assuming the feasible region is bounded so the ball cannot fall forever).

What are the forces acting on the ball in equilibrium?

  1. Gravity exerts a force on the ball equal to the gradient of the objective. Since the objective is $c_1x_1+\cdots+c_nx_n$, this force is $(c_1,\ldots,c_n)$.
  2. Each constraint can exert a normal force on the ball if it is touching the corresponding wall. This force acts perpendicular to the wall and points towards the feasible region. Thus, the constraint $a_{i1}x_1+\cdots+a_{in}x_n\le b_i$ exerts a force of the form $-y_i(a_{i1},\ldots,a_{in})$, where the unknown $y_i \geq 0$ determines the magnitude of the force, and is zero whenever the ball is not touching the corresponding wall.

Since the ball is in equilibrium, the forces sum to zero:

$$ (c_1,\ldots,c_n) - \sum_{i=1}^m y_i (a_{i1},\ldots,a_{in}) = 0 $$

where $y_i \geq 0$ for all $i = 1,\ldots,m$. Equivalently, we can also write this as

$$ \begin{array}{r@{\quad}rcl} y_1\times & a_{11}x_1+\cdots+a_{1n}x_n & \le & b_1 \\ y_2\times & a_{21}x_1+\cdots+a_{2n}x_n & \le & b_2 \\ \vdots & \vdots & & \vdots \\ y_m\times & a_{m1}x_1+\cdots+a_{mn}x_n & \le & b_m \\ \hline & c_1x_1+\cdots+c_nx_n & \le & \displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^m b_i y_i. \end{array} $$

Note that this tells us that $(y_1,\ldots,y_m)$ is a dual feasible solution: the weights are nonnegative, and the combined coefficient of each $x_j$ is exactly $c_j$, so all the dual constraints are satisfied with equality. Thus, $\sum_{i=1}^m b_i y_i$ is an upper bound on the primal optimal solution. If we can show that this must equal the primal optimal value we will have shown that strong duality holds.

Recall that the ball settles at the “lowest” point under gravity, where the objective is highest. Its equilibrium position, which we will denote $(x_1^\ast,\ldots,x_n^\ast)$, is therefore primal optimal. Any wall touching the ball corresponds to a tight constraint:

$$ a_{i1}x_1^\ast+\cdots+a_{in}x_n^\ast=b_i. $$

A strict inequality would mean there is still a gap between the ball and the wall. Any wall not touching the ball exerts no force, so its weight $y_i$ is zero. Therefore, either $y_i=0$ or the corresponding constraint is tight. In either case,

$$ y_i(a_{i1}x_1^\ast+\cdots+a_{in}x_n^\ast)=y_i b_i. $$

Thus, plugging $(x_1^\ast,\ldots,x_n^\ast)$ into the earlier display gets us

$$ \begin{array}{r@{\quad}rcl} y_1\times & a_{11}x_1^\ast+\cdots+a_{1n}x_n^\ast & = & b_1 \\ y_2\times & a_{21}x_1^\ast+\cdots+a_{2n}x_n^\ast & = & b_2 \\ \vdots & \vdots & & \vdots \\ y_m\times & a_{m1}x_1^\ast+\cdots+a_{mn}x_n^\ast & = & b_m \\ \hline & c_1x_1^\ast+\cdots+c_nx_n^\ast & = & \displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^m b_i y_i \end{array} $$

and showing that strong duality holds.

Force balance gives the coefficients on the left. On the right, only constraints that are tight at equilibrium have nonzero weights, so $\sum_{i=1}^my_i b_i=c_1x_1^*+\cdots+c_nx_n^*$.

This relationship between a constraint's slack and its weight is called complementary slackness. For the primal and dual LPs above, it gives the following optimality criterion.

Lemma (Complementary slackness). Let $x_1^*,\ldots,x_n^*$ be primal feasible and $y_1,\ldots,y_m$ be dual feasible. These solutions are both optimal if and only if

$$ \begin{aligned} y_i\left(b_i-\sum_{j=1}^n a_{ij}x_j^*\right)&=0 &&\text{for every }i=1,\ldots,m,\\ x_j^*\left(\sum_{i=1}^m a_{ij}y_i-c_j\right)&=0 &&\text{for every }j=1,\ldots,n. \end{aligned} $$

In words, a positive dual weight requires the corresponding primal constraint to be tight, and a positive primal variable requires the corresponding dual constraint to be tight.

The first condition is exactly the contact rule in our physical picture: a wall that the ball is not touching exerts no force. The second accounts for the coordinate walls $x_j\ge0$: if $x_j^*>0$, that wall exerts no force, so the other constraint forces must balance gravity in coordinate $j$.

To describe these forces explicitly, consider the linear objective $f(x_1,\ldots,x_n)=c_1x_1+\cdots+c_nx_n$. Its gradient is constant:

$$ \nabla f=(c_1,\ldots,c_n). $$

Thus, gravity has component $c_j$ in coordinate direction $x_j$. In our example, $f=x_1+2x_2$, so gravity points in the direction $(1,2)$.

Now consider a constraint $a_{i1}x_1+\cdots+a_{in}x_n\le b_i$. The vector $(a_{i1},\ldots,a_{in})$ is perpendicular to its wall and points toward increasing values of the left-hand side, so it points out of the allowed half-space. The wall pushes in the opposite direction, into the allowed half-space. We can therefore write its normal force as

$$ -y_i(a_{i1},\ldots,a_{in}),\qquad y_i\ge0. $$

Here $y_i$ is a nonnegative scale factor, not necessarily the force's magnitude, since the normal vector need not have length one. For example, the wall $-x_1+4x_2=24$ pushes in the direction $(1,-4)$. A wall can exert a force only when the ball is touching it, so $y_i=0$ whenever constraint $i$ is not tight. For this mathematical interpretation, we treat the ball as a point, so contact means equality in the constraint.

Here is what happens when the objective has no upper bound. Keep $x_1,x_2\ge0$ and the two corridor walls $2x_1-x_2\le5$ and $-2x_1+x_2\le4$. These walls run parallel to the objective’s gradient. Along $x_1=t$, $x_2=2t$, the objective $x_1+2x_2=5t$ grows without limit. Drop a ball below: it accelerates out of view and keeps moving, with no wall to stop it.

Farkas' Lemma: Certifying Infeasibility

So far, we have used weighted sums of constraints to certify that a solution is optimal. But what if there is no feasible solution at all? Can we give our boss a short certificate of that too?

Sometimes the contradiction is easy to see. For example, the constraints $x_1\le1$ and $-x_1\le-2$ require $x_1$ to be both at most $1$ and at least $2$. Adding them gives $0\le-1$, which is impossible. Farkas' lemma says that whenever a system of linear inequalities is infeasible, there is a weighted combination that exposes a contradiction like this.

Theorem (Farkas' lemma). For any real coefficients $a_{ij}$ and $b_i$, exactly one of the following holds:

  1. There exist $x_1,\ldots,x_n\ge0$ satisfying

    $$ a_{i1}x_1+\cdots+a_{in}x_n\le b_i \qquad\text{for every }i=1,\ldots,m. $$

  2. There exist $y_1,\ldots,y_m\ge0$ satisfying

    $$ \begin{aligned} a_{1j}y_1+\cdots+a_{mj}y_m&\ge0 &&\text{for every }j=1,\ldots,n,\\ b_1y_1+\cdots+b_my_m&<0. \end{aligned} $$

Why does the second alternative certify infeasibility? Multiply constraint $i$ by $y_i$ and add. Any feasible solution would have to satisfy

$$ 0\le\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(a_{1j}y_1+\cdots+a_{mj}y_m\right)x_j \le b_1y_1+\cdots+b_my_m<0, $$

a contradiction. The first inequality follows because both the combined coefficients and the variables are nonnegative. The last inequality is precisely what our certificate requires.

The remarkable part is that such a certificate always exists when the system is infeasible. Just as dual variables can certify optimality, nonnegative weights can certify that no feasible solution exists. Checking the certificate only requires multiplying and adding the given coefficients.
